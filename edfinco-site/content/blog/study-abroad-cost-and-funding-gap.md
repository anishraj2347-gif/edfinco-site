Before you compare a single loan option, it helps to know your number. Not just the tuition fee on your offer letter, but what the whole degree is likely to cost, and how much of that you still need to fund. That second number is your **funding gap**.

If you're the student, you probably know the university's figures. If you're the parent, you know the family's. So work through this together, step by step.

One rule throughout: use official, current figures wherever you can, from your university's website, your offer letter and government sources. This guide gives you the method, not the prices.

## Step 1: Tuition for the whole course

Make sure your tuition figure covers the whole course, not just the first year.

- Check whether the fee is quoted per year, per semester, per term or per credit.
- Multiply it out for the whole course. A one-year Master's and a two-year Master's are very different budgets.
- Add compulsory extras your university lists, such as enrolment deposits, student services fees, or lab and course fees.
- Check whether fees can rise in later years of the course.

## Step 2: Living costs, month by month

Living costs are harder to pin down, because they depend on your city, your housing and your habits. Build a monthly estimate that includes:

- Rent
- Food and groceries
- Local transport
- Utilities, phone and internet
- Books, supplies and course materials
- Personal costs, from clothes to the odd trip

Many universities publish an estimated cost of living for students. If your destination asks students to show a minimum amount of living funds for a study visa or permit, check that official figure too. Treat both as a starting point, not your full budget.

Then multiply your monthly figure by the number of months you'll actually be there, including holidays if you're staying on.

## Step 3: One-time and setup costs

These are easy to forget, and some of them fall due before any loan money is released, so plan how you'll pay for them.

- University application fees and test fees
- Visa or study permit fees and any related charges
- Flights, plus any trips home you're planning
- Health insurance, which many universities and countries require international students to have
- Your first accommodation deposit
- Setting up: bedding, kitchen basics, winter clothing and a laptop if you need one
- Bank and currency-conversion charges when you send money abroad, and any tax collected at source on the transfer (check the current rules with your bank)

## Step 4: Add a buffer

Fees change, exchange rates move and plans shift. A buffer gives you room for all three.

Choose a buffer you're comfortable with, as a percentage of your subtotal or a fixed amount. The less certain your figures, the bigger it should be. It also helps to convert foreign-currency costs at a cautious exchange rate rather than today's.

## Step 5: Subtract what you already have

Now list the money that's genuinely available:

- Family savings you're ready to commit to the degree
- Scholarships, bursaries or assistantships, but only those confirmed in writing
- Any other confirmed support, such as an employer sponsorship

Leave out anything uncertain, such as part-time work income. Work rules vary by country, and your studies come first.

## Put it all together

Here's the whole calculation as a simple worksheet. The letters are placeholders for your own figures, not real costs for any course or country.

1. Tuition and compulsory fees for the whole course: ₹A
2. Living costs (monthly estimate × number of months): ₹B
3. One-time and setup costs: ₹C
4. Subtotal: ₹A + ₹B + ₹C
5. Buffer: ₹D
6. Total estimated cost: subtotal + ₹D
7. Money you already have: ₹E
8. **Your estimated funding gap: total estimated cost minus ₹E**

Revisit your estimate whenever something changes, such as a new fee notice, a scholarship decision or a big move in the exchange rate.

## Before you take your number to a lender

Your funding gap is a starting point for a conversation with a lender, which will do its own assessment. Keep these in mind:

- **The lender decides the amount.** Eligibility, sanction amount, pricing and fees are the lender's call, and the amount offered may differ from your estimate.
- **Not every lender covers every cost.** Ask what each lender will and won't fund, such as living costs, travel or insurance.
- **You may need to contribute.** Some lenders expect the family to fund part of the cost themselves, often called margin money.
- **Money may arrive in stages.** A lender may release funds in instalments, and may pay tuition directly to the university, so match your plan to its schedule.
- **Interest can build up while you study.** Ask each lender how interest works during your course.

## Mistakes worth avoiding

- Budgeting for tuition only
- Using last year's fees or an old exchange rate
- Counting scholarships or part-time income that isn't confirmed
- Skipping the buffer because the total already feels big
